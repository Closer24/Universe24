\title[A discrete spacetime of events in whole numbers]{A discrete spacetime of events in whole numbers: why clocks slow, why nothing outruns light, and why time has a direction only where something was measured}
\author*{\fnm{Alon} \sur{Gonen}\,\orcidlink{0009-0008-2701-4599}}\email{alon@defounder.ai}
\affil{\orgname{Independent researcher}, \orgaddress{\city{Haifa}, \country{Israel}}}

\abstract{In our view nature cannot be continuous: in quantum mechanics things come in whole pieces, and a measurement gives one outcome that cannot be unmade. We propose a discrete board of time events: Nodes of whole numbers, each speaking to six neighbours once per tick, one step per tick, and a gravity content setting each Node's rate; a click is a detector's reading of the board. Each tick a Node adds its neighbours' weighted numbers, subtracts its number of one tick before, divides once, remainder kept: every step runs back. From that rule we find one bound on everything on the board, no body outrunning light and, given a two-way reading by light, the known addition of speeds, clocks slowed near matter, moving clocks slowed in the known form at first order, light slowed near matter and, in form, bent and delayed, and a board time that no body reads. None of it gives time a direction; that comes only at a click: a detector takes one whole piece, records it, wipes the rest, one step per tick. With the record the board runs back through the click; without it the backward run fails where the wiping reached, no faster than one step per tick. On the board, the direction of time is the ledger of what was measured: a place, a speed, a size. One run shows it. We propose nature's arrow appears this way. Everything here is in the public repository and can be run by anyone.}

\keywords{arrow of time, reversible rule, whole numbers, measurement, detectors, erasure}

\section{The problem and the answer}\label{sec:problem}

Physics today has no agreed answer to an old question \cite{loschmidt1876,boltzmann1877,boltzmann1896}. Every basic law we know, one small piece of particle decay aside, works the same forward and backward. Yet everything we see goes one way. A cup breaks and never un-breaks.

One answer asks for a very special start \cite{boltzmann1877,boltzmann1896,rovelli2019}. Collapse models add a random jolt to the law, with a rate and a length put in \cite{grw1986}, or a time set by gravity's own energy \cite{penrose1996}. Decoherence places the arrow where the surroundings register a system \cite{zurek2003,joos1985}; that the arrow is what can be recorded was argued and answered for quantum systems \cite{maccone2009,jennings2010}. The cost of erasing information has been counted \cite{landauer1961,bennett1973,bennett1982,zurek1989}; that the loss sits where no copy of what was erased is kept is an old thesis \cite{bennett1982}, and new here is the exact count on a board, a grid of sites called Nodes, in three dimensions and in whole numbers. This paper too adds three things beside the step, what every Node does at each tick, the board's one beat: a toss, which is a weighted draw, a wiping, which overwrites numbers, and a write into the taker. What differs is this: the rule has no direction of time, the step stays exactly reversible in whole numbers, the toss runs back with its record, and the loss is counted exactly and bounded to one step per tick.

The claim: On a board of Nodes stepped by one whole-number rule, no step has a direction of time. A direction appears only where a detector we place, one Node or a few with a record, clicks (takes something from the numbers and records it) and wipes, and only for whoever has no record of what was wiped. The failure of the backward run then has a place, a size, the wiped count, and a spread never above one step per tick, each a whole number or a count of them.

If the world is built as such a board, this is where the direction of time in nature comes from: not in the law, but in the ledger of what was measured. We propose that it is so.

\section{The board}\label{sec:board}

\textit{What we assume.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Nature is discrete: a board of events, Nodes at ticks, with nothing between them. It looks continuous not because it is, but because it is made of very many small parts and we measure from far: Section~\ref{sec:time} puts the distance between Nodes under about $10^{-25}$~m, far below anything measured.
\item A Node reads its six neighbours (front and back, left and right, up and down) once per tick and writes only itself; nothing passes faster than one step per tick (the speed of light follows, below).
\item What we see is clicks, not the board.
\item Every number at a Node is a whole number; the step is exact, the remainder (what is left over from the division) kept.
\item Every Node does one simple act, the step of equation~(\ref{eq:rule}), the same for light and matter; each pattern of these carries two whole numbers, a top and a bottom; the kinds differ in how the two stand.
\item Light is the kind with the top equal to the bottom: it moves and does not turn in place. Matter is the kind with the top below the bottom: it turns in place even at rest.
\item One kind, gravity, has a content at the Node, a count of units held there, fixed through a run, that sets the Node's rate, a whole number, $q_0$ at an empty Node, $q_0$ chosen, which sets how much of a swing it advances per tick (equation~(\ref{eq:turn})), a swing being the numbers' going up, down and back.
\item The click, put in by hand: a detector, one Node or a few with a record, takes one whole piece, the smallest amount a detector can take, records it, and wipes the rest.
\end{enumerate}
We conjecture a fourth kind, binding, that holds a body's Nodes together. This paper does not use it. It is what would let a detector be a body of the board, producing its own clicks and keeping its own record (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). Everything else in this paper follows from these by the rule, or is counted on the board; where something is put in by hand, we say so.

Each Node has six neighbours. One event can touch another only through its six neighbours: a number held at a Node is read by its neighbours at the next tick, and by nobody else. Space and time on the board are read off these connections and off the clicks. One tick for every Node at once is what physics calls a preferred frame (Section~\ref{sec:time}, on what a body can read of it). Figure~\ref{fig:board} draws the board.

A Node keeps three whole numbers for each of light and matter: now, before, and the remainder left over from the last division.

Each tick, every Node does the same thing at the same time. It reads the six numbers its neighbours hold now, each with a fixed weight; adds its own number now, with its own weight, which is zero where no gravity content stands; takes away its number from one tick before; and adds the old remainder. Then it divides the total by a fixed whole number $w$, keeping the quotient as its next number and the remainder. This is the rule's definition, taken from \cite{first}; nothing in it is derived. As one line, with $a_{\mathrm{next}}$ the next number, $a_j$ the number arriving from the neighbour $j$, $R_j$ the weight of that neighbour, $S$ the Node's own weight, $a_{\mathrm{now}}$ and $a_{\mathrm{before}}$ its two numbers, $r$ the old remainder and $r'$ the new one:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
\boxed{\,w\,a_{\mathrm{next}} + r' = \sum_{\text{six}} R_j\,a_j + S\,a_{\mathrm{now}} - w\,a_{\mathrm{before}} + r, \quad 0 \le r' < w.\,}
\end{equation}
The right side is one whole number. The left side says how it is split: $w$ times the next number, plus a remainder smaller than $w$. The numbers are whole numbers with sign; the quotient is the largest whole number not above the total over $w$, so the remainder lies in $0 \le r' < w$ for a total of either sign. A rule of the form ``next = a function of the present numbers, minus the number before'' runs back for any function \cite{toffoli1990,fredkin1990}. Ours is one such function, exact in whole numbers by the kept remainder. Boards of whole numbers have been proposed before \cite{zuse1969,wolfram2002,thooft2016}, and a world that is mathematics \cite{tegmark2008}.

\textit{The kinds of pattern.} A kind of pattern is chosen by two whole numbers, a top and a bottom number. With $n$ units of gravity content at the Node the rule's definition says that each unit multiplies the rate by $(1 - 1/q_0)$, so the rate is $q_0\,(1 - 1/q_0)^n$.

The rule's definition gives the three weights at a Node from the two numbers and the rate. The divisor is $w = 6\,\mathrm{bottom}\,q_0^2$. The rate squared over $q_0$ is the Node's reach toward each neighbour, and each neighbour's weight is twice the top number times that reach squared, $R_j = 2\,\mathrm{top}\,(\mathrm{rate}^2/q_0)^2$. The own weight is $S = 12\,(\mathrm{bottom}\,q_0^2 - (\mathrm{bottom} - \mathrm{top})\,\mathrm{rate}^2) - 6R_j$. At the empty rate, where the rate is $q_0$, the three are $6\,\mathrm{bottom}\,q_0^2$, $2\,\mathrm{top}\,q_0^2$ and 0: the own weight is zero wherever no gravity content stands. Two of the rule's whole numbers are rounded: the rate, the exact fraction $q_0\,(1 - 1/q_0)^n$ rounded half up once, and the reach, the rate squared over $q_0$, rounded half up once (rounded half up: the whole part of the quotient, plus one where the remainder is at least half the divisor); the weights $R_j$, $S$ and $w$ are then exact products of whole numbers. Equations~(\ref{eq:rate}) to~(\ref{eq:light}) use the unrounded forms, which the rounding misses by at most a half in the rate and the reach. The pair has $1 \le \mathrm{top} \le \mathrm{bottom}$, $q_0$ is a whole number above 1, the content $n$ a whole number at or above 0, and $w$ is above 0. For light, top equals bottom, so the six weights add up to twice $w$: the Node follows the mean of its neighbours' numbers and nothing holds it in place. For matter, top is below bottom, so the six weights add up to less than twice $w$, and the shortfall turns the numbers in place even at rest.

\textit{Every step runs back.} After the step, the numbers that were read are still on the board: each Node's old number is now its before number. Take $w$ times the next number, add the new remainder, and subtract everything that was read. What is left is the old remainder minus $w$ times the old before number. Divide that by $w$: the quotient, its sign turned, is the old before number, and the remainder is the old remainder, because it is smaller than $w$. This follows from the rule alone.

\textit{The speed of light.} A Node speaks only to its six neighbours, once per tick. So no number, no pattern and no news moves more than one step per tick: one bound for everything on the board. The wiping after a click is made to run at exactly that limit, outrunning light along a row and matching it along a body diagonal. Measured in a straight line, light's speed follows from equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) with light's weights, each a third of $w$ and the own weight 0. A long pattern, many Nodes from one highest number to the next (crest to crest), then obeys ``next plus before equals twice the mean of the six neighbours'', whose speed squared for long patterns is one third: about 0.58 Node per tick. We write $c$ for that speed.

The six neighbours sit as the six faces of a cube. The turnings and mirror images that leave the cube as it was form the cube's symmetry group, of order 48, the order being how many it holds. The step treats the six neighbours alike, so the step commutes with the group, remainders included: a symmetry then a step is a step then that symmetry. A long pattern's speed is therefore the same in every direction, because the group's only invariant of second degree, a number every symmetry leaves as it is, is the sphere's, the sum of the squares along the three axes; the cube enters at fourth degree (Section~\ref{sec:time})\footnote{Counted in steps: one step per tick along a body diagonal, 0.816 along a face diagonal and 0.577 along a row; in length the same.}, and only a short pattern feels the cube. Under the group the rate, the content, the count and the piece are scalars, unchanged by every symmetry; the board's momentum (Section~\ref{sec:click}) and what the click writes into the taker are vectors, turning with the axes; the wiped region and a body of one Node are invariant, each mapped onto itself. All of this follows from the rule and the group together.

Nature shows one top speed for light and for every signal, the same in every direction and the same whatever sent the light \cite{brecher1977,michelson1887}. The board has one bound for everything, one step per tick, and light at $c = 1/\sqrt3$ step per tick along a row, the same speed in length in every direction for long patterns (footnote 1). The bound and $c$ are not one speed. In the board's frame two patterns of light meeting head-on close at $2c$; that is nobody's reading (Section~\ref{sec:time}). The wiping is carried Node to Node at the bound: a detector it reaches reads its arrival, the one bit the draw fixed, sooner than light from the click could have told it, $\sqrt3$ times along a row. News on the board moves at the bound; light, and every body of the matter kind, slower (Section~\ref{sec:time}). That is the title's ``nothing'': no body outruns light; news on the board may. \section{The click}\label{sec:click}

Two rules run the board: the step, which is reversible, and the click, which we declare, with a detector, a counting stretch, a toss and a write. A piece is one count in the detector's books, the count it keeps of pieces; on the board it is carried by a pattern of numbers moving from Node to Node. 

In the run each detector was an atom of two Nodes with two states, low and high, counting over a fixed stretch of ticks. Over the stretch the detector $i$ adds up the numbers at its Nodes, each multiplied by one of two fixed up-and-down number sequences of its own, giving two sums, $X_i$ and $Y_i$. The size of the two sums together, times a declared strength $g$, over a fixed scale $M$, is the detector's turn, an angle measured as a part of one full swing of the detector's own numbers; this is put in by hand, and the detector's weight $W_i$ in the toss is the square of the sine of that turn:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weight}
\mathrm{turn}_i = \frac{g\,\sqrt{X_i^2 + Y_i^2}}{M}, \qquad W_i = \sin^2(\mathrm{turn}_i).
\end{equation}
In the run the weight is computed as a ratio of two whole numbers: the detector keeps one whole number for each of its two states, the whole of a fixed unit in the low state at first and 0 in the high; at a stretch's end the two sums of equation~(\ref{eq:weight}) give the turn, the root taken once as the largest whole number whose square is at most the sum, the pair of numbers is turned by that turn in whole numbers, and the weight is the high state's number squared over the sum of the two squares; the sine of equation~(\ref{eq:weight}) is that ratio's form, and the toss is drawn from the ratios outside the board. At the end of a stretch one toss is made over every detector that read the piece. The no-click weight $W_0$ comes from the books: what is left of one piece, $U$, one at the first stretch, lowered by the weights of every earlier stretch in which no click came, less the weights now, never below zero; each detector's chance $P_i$ is its weight over the sum of all the weights, the no-click one included:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chance}
W_0 = \max\Big(\min(U, 1) - \sum_{\text{all}} W,\ 0\Big), \qquad P_i = \frac{W_i}{W_0 + \sum_{\text{all}} W}.
\end{equation}
This is a counter on whole numbers, reported as fractions, read against the detector's two sequences. No toss is made while the count stands at zero.

If the toss says take, the click happens. One whole piece is taken: the count goes from 1 to 0, the atom from its low state to its high, and its own numbers are rewritten. Then the wiping begins at the Node that took the piece and reaches one step farther each tick, the board's limit. At each wiped Node the rule is given the chosen value at one neighbour for two ticks, so it writes the piece's now to zero at the first tick and its before to zero at the second; the remainder stays as the rule computes it.

The detector's record holds four kinds of entry: the numbers its own Nodes held before they were rewritten, the two given values at every wiped Node, how far the wiping has reached, and one line saying that a piece was taken, and when.

With the record, every click runs back; the argument is short. Each act of a click is a rewriting or a wiping, and each has one way back: put the old number back from the record. Between the acts the board steps by the rule, which has one way back (Section~\ref{sec:board}). A chain of steps that each have one way back has one way back: undo the last, then the one before, down to the first. So the whole run, click included, runs backward number for number. The weights are read from the gravity content, which nothing here moves, so the same weights serve forward and back.

Without the wiped numbers in the record, the board cannot be run back; one may ask where it fails first. The backward run has the board's numbers as they stand and the record, nothing else; whoever holds the start and the toss's seed holds a copy of what was wiped and can rerun the board forward; the claim is about running back from the board as it stands, with that copy and the wiping's record both gone. The first step back starts where the wiping last reached. The division there needs the number that was wiped out, and reads the replacement instead. So the result is wrong at those Nodes. Each further step back, a Node next to a wrong Node reads a wrong number and may go wrong itself (wrong numbers can also cancel), and no Node farther than one step can be touched in one tick; so the wrong set grows by at most one step on each side per tick. The rule allows no more.

\textit{What the wiping overwrote, counted.} Two whole numbers per wiped Node, its now and its before, and two at the taker. The wiped set is a ball counted in steps: from the taking Node the wiping reaches the Nodes at $t$ steps at its $t$-th tick, and a step being one connection, the layer at $t$ steps holds $4t^2 + 2$ Nodes for $t \ge 1$ and one Node at $t = 0$, so by the rule's one step per tick the ball within $t$ steps holds, counting the layers up:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ball}
N(t) = \frac{4t^3 + 6t^2 + 8t + 3}{3}, \qquad 1,\ 7,\ 25,\ 63,\ 129,\ \dots
\end{equation}
In a tube, a board whose sides hold zero, $L$ Nodes wide, the ball fills the width and then runs along the tube, naming $2L^2$ Nodes and $4L^2$ values per tick.

\textit{What the step keeps.} The step keeps one more thing, exactly without the remainder where the rate is the same at every Node: a momentum of the board along each axis, and we shall need it at the click. Write $\mathrm{now}_k$ and $\mathrm{before}_k$ for the two numbers of the Node $k$, $k+a$ and $k-a$ for its neighbours along the axis $a$, and $F_{kj} = \mathrm{now}_k\,\mathrm{before}_j - \mathrm{before}_k\,\mathrm{now}_j$ for the flow into the Node $k$ from its neighbour $j$, a cross-difference of the kind called a Wronskian; by equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) the board's momentum along $a$, the flow in from one side less the flow in from the other, summed over every Node, is kept without the remainder where the rate is the same at every Node and no number stands at the board's two ends along that axis (an end, holding zero, exchanges momentum with the board; in the run the light never reached them):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:momentum}
P_a = \sum_k \big(F_{k,k-a} - F_{k,k+a}\big).
\end{equation}
Why is it kept? Where the rate is the same at every Node, equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) without the remainder reads $\mathrm{next}_k + \mathrm{before}_k = \sum_j (R_j/w)\,a_j + (S/w)\,\mathrm{now}_k$, with the same weight from a Node to its neighbour as from the neighbour back; put next for now and now for before in equation~(\ref{eq:momentum}): the weighted terms cancel in pairs, one each way along the axis, and the terms in before give $P_a$ back. With the remainder the step changes $P_a$ by less than $2\sum_k |\mathrm{now}_{k-a} - \mathrm{now}_{k+a}|$, each remainder below $w$, and the changes add up over ticks. The books keep the count of pieces; that is the books' rule, not the step's. At a click the taker is given the whole piece (in the run one piece lay as two halves of light, Section~\ref{sec:run}): its count, and the momentum of what it took as read at its Nodes, written as a turn of the taker's numbers that grows from Node to Node along the axis; the turn carries that momentum where the taker can hold it, else the largest it holds, a quarter swing: the click writes into the taker four fifths of what the half that reached it carried, as its two Nodes read it (Section~\ref{sec:run}); the wiping of the other half's tail is where the board's momentum is lost (Section~\ref{sec:run}). Because the piece is one whole, the count, kept by the books (above), falls to zero at the click, and no toss is made while it stands at zero: a second detector's chance is zero from that tick, wherever it stands. The count's fall reaches every detector at the same tick through the books, outside the board; the books' acts, the toss's sum and the count's fall, are the only things here not bound to one step per tick. Two detectors that read one piece are bound by it, and we say they are tied; on the board nothing carries the tie. The tie is the count's conservation in whole pieces.

\section{What time is on the board}\label{sec:time}

A body's clock is how many swings it completes in so many ticks. A body, a pattern of numbers on the board, an atom say, cannot see the board's tick; it counts its own swings and its own clicks, and that is its time. One body's count differs from another's in pace for two reasons, and in direction for a third.

\textit{Where it stands.} With $n$ units of gravity content the rule of Section~\ref{sec:board} gives the rate:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rate}
\mathrm{rate} = q_0\,(1 - 1/q_0)^{n}.
\end{equation}
From the rule it follows that a Node whose six neighbours hold its own number steps, by equation~(\ref{eq:rule}), to next plus before equals now times twice the cosine of the turn, the angle its numbers turn through in one tick, a part of one swing; the neighbours' weight cancels in that cosine. In the words top, bottom and rate, with $q_0$ the empty rate, the rule gives the turn:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:turn}
\cos(\mathrm{turn}) = 1 - \Big(1 - \frac{\mathrm{top}}{\mathrm{bottom}}\Big)\Big(\frac{\mathrm{rate}}{q_0}\Big)^{2}.
\end{equation}
Section~\ref{sec:run} counts it on the board; this turn is the Node's, per tick, and the detector's turn of equation~(\ref{eq:weight}) is another angle; angles are in radians, a full swing $2\pi$. Equations~(\ref{eq:turn}) to~(\ref{eq:slow}) are the rule's linear form, the remainder's term $(r - r')/w$ dropped; that term is below one unit of the Node's number per tick, so a pattern of height $A$ follows them to within about $1/A$ per tick, $T/A$ over $T$ ticks; at heights of a few units the rule leaves them (a Node holding 1 may fall to 0 in one step). Nature's clocks lower in gravity run slow: the measured shift of light climbing in gravity \cite{pound1960} gives, to first order in the potential $\Phi$, the known measure of how deep in gravity a clock sits, the rate $1 + \Phi/c^2$ \cite{einstein1911}. Ours, from equation~(\ref{eq:turn}) at small content, is linear in the content as the known one is in the potential: the swings fall as $1 - s\,n/q_0$ with the slope $s = 2\tan(\mathrm{turn}_0/2)/\mathrm{turn}_0$, $\mathrm{turn}_0$ the turn at the empty rate, nearing 1 as top nears bottom. The forms agree at first order, and $s$ is 1 to the order counted below, at the tick's bound.

\textit{Light near matter.} Near matter light still follows the mean of its neighbours, but with the share $(\mathrm{rate}/q_0)^4/3$ each and the rest on itself; by equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) a long pattern of light then moves at the speed $c(n)$ at a Node of content $n$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:light}
c(n) = c\,\Big(\frac{\mathrm{rate}}{q_0}\Big)^{2} = c\,(1 - 1/q_0)^{2n},
\end{equation}
about $c\exp(-2n/q_0)$, while the swings of equation~(\ref{eq:turn}) fall as $1 - s\,n/q_0$. In other words light slows twice as strongly as the rate, $2 : s$ against the clock; the known ratio is $2 : 1$. The two powers are the definition's, the rate to the fourth in $R_j$ against the second in $S$ (Section~\ref{sec:board}), so the board meets the ratio and nothing here derives it; $s$ is 1 for nature's clocks, as shown below; we counted the speed on a row (Section~\ref{sec:run}). Both ratios are the board's own, read in its ticks and Nodes (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). Where the content changes slowly from Node to Node a long pattern follows the ray's law, that a path bends toward where it moves slower: the ray turns toward the content by $2/q_0$ times the difference $\Delta n$ of $n$ across the ray, per Node, and over a path of length $\ell$ it is late by $(2/(c q_0))\int n\,d\ell$, to first order in $n/q_0$, the forms of the bending and the delay seen near the Sun \cite{dyson1920,shapiro1964} (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). The rate of equation~(\ref{eq:rate}) is above zero at every $n$, so by the form no clock stops and light leaves every Node, only slower: the form has no horizon. In whole numbers the reach rounds to zero once the rate falls below $\sqrt{q_0/2}$, at a content near $(q_0/2)\ln(2q_0)$: such a Node reads nothing from its neighbours, so no pattern enters it and the wiping's write, given through a neighbour, cannot reach it; its neighbours still read it, and its own number is not frozen: a matter Node swings on alone at the slow turn equation~(\ref{eq:turn}) gives at that rate; a light Node, its turn 0 there, changes by the same amount each tick, next $= 2\,\mathrm{now} - \mathrm{before}$.

\textit{How it moves.} A moving body is a pattern of numbers handed on from Node to Node, a fixed distance each tick, and each Node it reaches is a fixed part $p$ of a swing behind the one it left (seen from far, the pattern looks like a wave), so part of each swing goes to the passing: a Node the pattern passes through swings faster than it would with the body at rest, but the number at the body's middle swings less, by the part the handing takes. By equation~(\ref{eq:rule}), such a Node's turn, the middle's speed $v$ and the middle's turn per tick, its swings per tick being this over $2\pi$, as Section~\ref{sec:run}'s counts have it, are:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moving}
\cos(\mathrm{turn}) = \frac{\mathrm{top}}{\mathrm{bottom}}\Big(1 - \frac{1 - \cos p}{3}\Big), \qquad v = \frac{d\,\mathrm{turn}}{d p}, \qquad \text{middle's turn} = \mathrm{turn} - p\,v.
\end{equation}
Nature's moving clocks run slow: the measured light of fast-moving atoms \cite{ives1938} gives the rate $(1 - v^2/c^2)^{1/2}$ \cite{einstein1905}, to first order $1 - v^2/(2c^2)$. Ours, from equation~(\ref{eq:moving}) at small speed, is $1 - v^2/(2c_m^2)$ with $c_m^2 = \mathrm{turn}_0 \cot(\mathrm{turn}_0)/3$, $c_m$ the speed that stands for $c$ in the matter kind's own form, light's $c^2 = 1/3$ only as top nears bottom. This is the branch of long patterns, $p$ small; for short ones the middle counts more than at rest: the two counts cross at $p$ about two at the worked pair, where the pattern moves at $0.32c$, and nearer $p = 0$ as top nears bottom, where it moves near $c$; the result is the branch below that crossing. The form is the known one and the coefficient is the pattern's own, $c_m$ in place of $c$; how near $c_m$ comes to $c$ for a clock of nature, the tick's bound tells below. The board's form departs at fourth order by $2\,\mathrm{turn}_0/\sin(2\,\mathrm{turn}_0)$, 1 as top nears bottom. From equation~(\ref{eq:moving}) a body of the matter kind never moves faster than $c$: its speed squared stays below $1/3$ for every top below bottom. A body at the speed $v_A$ that sends light to another, at $v_B$, and counts the returns by its own swings, with light as its ruler, reads the other's speed as $u = (v_B - v_A)/(1 - v_A v_B/c^2)$, below $c$, exactly at every pair, for long flashes on the empty board, the factor of the body's own count cancelling in the ratio; at light's $c$, not the pattern's own $c_m$. On the board light runs at $c$; the first body sends two flashes along their common line, a gap apart; the second, moving at $v_B$, meets them a gap $(c - v_A)/(c - v_B)$ times as long, and the two returns reach the first a gap $(c + v_B)/(c + v_A)$ times as long as that. Its own count scales both gaps by one factor, which cancels in their ratio. It reads the distance as $c$ times half the time between sending and return, and the time as their mean, so it reads the speed $c\,(\text{return gap} - \text{sending gap})/(\text{return gap} + \text{sending gap}) = (v_B - v_A)/(1 - v_A v_B/c^2)$, below $c$ whenever both are. That the count at the moving middle is what the body's clicks read, we propose (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}). If so, a moving clock counts fewer seconds.


\textit{How short the tick is.} By equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) with light's weights a long pattern moves at $c$; a short one, with the part $p$ of a swing from Node to Node, follows equation~(\ref{eq:moving}) with top equal to bottom and moves a little slower, along an axis by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slow}
v = c\,\Big(1 - \frac{p^2}{12} + \dots\Big),
\end{equation}
along a face diagonal by $c\,(1 - p^2/48 + \dots)$, and not at all along a body diagonal, where the turn is $p/\sqrt3$ at every $p$. Why do the three differ? The group's first invariant beyond the sphere's, $\sum_a d_a^2 = 1$, is $\sum_a d_a^4$ for the unit direction $\mathbf d$ with $d_a$ its part on the axis $a$, and a short pattern's slowing goes as $(3\sum_a d_a^4 - 1)/24$ times $p^2$: $1/12$ on an axis, $1/48$ on a face diagonal, $0$ on a body diagonal, $1/30$ averaged over directions; this follows from the rule. Nature's light shows no such slowing. The light of one distant burst, seen up to the energy $E = 1$~TeV, arrived with its speed $v$ changed by a part $|\delta v/v|$ below $3.8 \times 10^{-16}$; written as $\tfrac32\,(E/E_{\mathrm{QG},2})^2$, with $E_{\mathrm{QG},2}$ the scale in which such a slowing is written (not an energy at which it would equal the speed), this puts $E_{\mathrm{QG},2}$ above $6.3 \times 10^{10}$~GeV at 95 per cent confidence for light slowed, the board's case, the burst's own lags fitted beside it \cite{acciari2020}, one bound of this kind and not the tightest. A second burst, seen to 31~GeV, gives $1.3 \times 10^{11}$~GeV \cite{vasileiou2013}, which would halve the lengths below; a third, seen to 13~TeV \cite{lhaaso2023b}, gives $6.9 \times 10^{11}$~GeV at 95 per cent confidence \cite{lhaaso2024}, eleven times the first. The three stand $64^\circ$, $71^\circ$ and $130^\circ$ apart on the sky (from the positions reported in \cite{acciari2020,vasileiou2013,lhaaso2023b}); the body diagonals stand $70.5^\circ$, $109.5^\circ$ or, at the two ends of one diagonal, $180^\circ$ apart, so the pair at $130^\circ$ fits no two of them within five degrees, and no turn of the board hides all three near them; bounds of this kind are reviewed in \cite{mattingly2005}. Write $p = 2\pi\,\ell_0/\lambda$, with $\ell_0$ the distance between neighbouring Nodes and $\lambda$ the light's length from one crest to the next, $1.24 \times 10^{-18}$~m at 1~TeV; $\hbar$ is the constant that turns a rate of swinging into an energy, $E = 2\pi\hbar\nu$ for $\nu$ swings per second. Then $\ell_0$ is shorter than $3\sqrt2\,\hbar c/E_{\mathrm{QG},2} = 1.3 \times 10^{-26}$~m if that light ran along an axis of the board, and shorter than $1.1 \times 10^{-25}$~m in every direction more than five degrees from a body diagonal (the bound weakens toward it, eightfold at $5^\circ$, fortyfold at $1^\circ$, and is absent on it); the direction of the burst on the board we do not know. Light moves $0.577$ of that distance per tick, so the tick, the discreteness of time, is shorter than $\sqrt6\,\hbar/E_{\mathrm{QG},2} = 2.6 \times 10^{-35}$~s along an axis and than $2.2 \times 10^{-34}$~s in every direction more than five degrees from a body diagonal; with the three bursts $64^\circ$ to $130^\circ$ apart, at least one lies more than five degrees from every body diagonal however the board is turned, so $2.2 \times 10^{-34}$~s holds in every way the board may lie (the tightest bound would give $2 \times 10^{-36}$~s on an axis). A clock of nature turns through $\nu\tau$ of a swing per tick, $2\pi\nu\tau$ in radians, with $\tau$ the tick's length in seconds; taking a body's swing rate at rest as its rest energy over $2\pi\hbar$, an identification we add, the inner clock proposed before any board \cite{debroglie1925}, the electron's $1.24 \times 10^{20}$ swings per second and $\tau$ under $2.2 \times 10^{-34}$~s give $\mathrm{turn}_0$ under $1.8 \times 10^{-13}$, so top over bottom, $\cos(\mathrm{turn}_0)$, lies within $2 \times 10^{-26}$ of 1, $s$ within $3 \times 10^{-27}$ of 1 and $c_m^2$ within $4 \times 10^{-27}$ of $1/3$; nature's matter kind then has a bottom above $6 \times 10^{25}$; the proton, 1,836 times faster, within $6 \times 10^{-20}$, $10^{-20}$ and $2 \times 10^{-20}$. For every clock up to a uranium atom's rest rate the board's coefficients are the known ones, $1 - v^2/(2c^2)$ and $2 : 1$, if the middle's count is the body's time: $s = 1$ within $5 \times 10^{-16}$ and the moving clock's coefficient within $2 \times 10^{-15}$ ($c_m$ within $10^{-15}$ of $c$); $s - 1$ is $3 \times 10^{-27}$ at the electron's rate, $10^{-20}$ at the proton's, $3 \times 10^{-17}$ at the iron nucleus's, $2 \times 10^{-16}$ at a caesium atom's, $5 \times 10^{-16}$ at uranium's, and the moving clock's coefficient departs by four times as much, $\mathrm{turn}_0^2/3$. A click is written in one tick; the stretch over which a detector reads a piece is a count it declares, a whole number of half swings, or many swings: over such a stretch the size of the two sums of equation~(\ref{eq:weight}) no longer depends on where in the swing it began.

\begin{framed}\noindent\textit{A prediction.} If light of higher energy is ever seen to arrive later than light of lower energy from the same distant source, beyond what the source itself does, the board says how. The delay grows as the square of the energy, equation~(\ref{eq:slow}), and never the other way: on the board no light is faster than $c$. The delay differs by direction in the cube's pattern, set in the board's axes, which the data would place on the sky, $1/12$ along an axis, $1/48$ along a face diagonal, none along a body diagonal; and its size fixes the tick. Of the delay only the part that grows with the distance to the source is the board's; a source's own lag does not grow so, and sources at several distances tell the two apart. That part, found at the confidence the field uses, 95 per cent, growing in proportion to the energy, or with light arriving early, or the same in every direction once sources in enough directions are compared, would refute the board. The numbers above are a bound from a measurement, not a prediction; the form of the slowing is the prediction.\end{framed}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The claim: A board of Nodes, each speaking only with its six neighbours once per tick and stepped by one whole-number rule, has no direction of time: every step runs back exactly. The direction appears only where detectors we place click: the wiping removes the piece's numbers, and for anyone without a record of them (in the run, our log) the backward run fails where the wiping reached; the failure has a place, a speed never above one step per tick, and a size, a count of whole numbers. By the rule no body forgets.

\textit{Why a board like this agrees with what is seen.} Nothing below was fitted: the eight assumptions and the rule's definition of Section~\ref{sec:board}, its weights with their powers of the rate, are taken as given, and what follows comes from them and from what each item names as put in or assumed, nothing else (Table~\ref{tab:seen}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item One bound for everything. A Node speaks only to its six neighbours, once per tick, so nothing on the board, light, matter or news, moves more than one step per tick (the books' count is not on the board); equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) with light's weights gives long patterns of light the speed squared $c^2 = 1/3$ of that bound, the same in every direction [derived from the rule's linear form]. A body of the matter kind never outruns light. From equation~(\ref{eq:moving}) a body's speed squared stays below one third for every top below bottom: $v^2 < 1/3 = c^2$ [derived from the rule's linear form]. By two-way light no body reads another as faster than light. A body that sends light to another and counts the returns by its own swings, with light as its ruler, reads the other's speed as $u = (v_B - v_A)/(1 - v_A v_B/c^2)$, the known addition of speeds at light's $c$, below $c$ at every pair [light at $c$ from the rule; the two-way reading put in by hand; exact].
\item Clocks run slow near matter. Each unit of gravity content at a Node multiplies its rate by $(1 - 1/q_0)$, equation~(\ref{eq:rate}) [the rule's definition]; by equation~(\ref{eq:turn}) the swings then fall as $1 - s\,n/q_0$ with $s = 2\tan(\mathrm{turn}_0/2)/\mathrm{turn}_0$: linear in the content, as the known rate $1 + \Phi/c^2$ is linear in the potential [linear in the content as any smooth rule is at first order; $s$ derived from the rule's linear form, the remainder's term dropped; counted on the board (Section~\ref{sec:run}); the content not mapped to the potential].
\item Moving clocks run slow. A moving pattern spends part of each swing on the passing, equation~(\ref{eq:moving}), and its middle completes $1 - v^2/(2c_m^2)$ of the swings at rest, with $c_m^2 = \mathrm{turn}_0\cot(\mathrm{turn}_0)/3$, $c_m$ the speed that stands for $c$ in the matter kind's own form: the known form at first order, $c_m$ nearing $c$ as top nears bottom; at fourth order the board departs by $2\,\mathrm{turn}_0/\sin(2\,\mathrm{turn}_0)$ [derived from the rule's linear form, the remainder's term dropped; counted on the board (Section~\ref{sec:run}); $s = 1$ within $5 \times 10^{-16}$ and $c_m = c$ within $10^{-15}$ (Section~\ref{sec:time}); that the middle's count is the body's time, proposed].
\item Time has a direction only where something was measured. The click is put in: the weight the square of the sine of the turn, equation~(\ref{eq:weight}), the chance the weight over the sum, equation~(\ref{eq:chance}) [put in by hand; run; counted over seeds]. The wiping then reaches one step farther each tick, two whole numbers per wiped Node, $N(t)$ Nodes within $t$ steps by equation~(\ref{eq:ball}), $2L^2$ Nodes per tick in a full tube of width $L$ [the wiping's reach, one step per tick, declared with the click; $N(t)$ counted; the failure's spread derived from the rule; shown in the run]. Without the record the backward run fails where the wiping last reached, and the failure spreads never above one step per tick each way [derived from the rule; shown in the run]: the arrow's place, its size, the wiped count $N(t)$, and its spread, never above one step per tick, each a whole number or a count of them.
\end{enumerate}
No body reads the one time. The board has one tick for every Node, a preferred frame, but no body reads it; two bodies disagree in pace for two reasons, where they stand (item 2) and how they move (item 3), and in direction for a third, what they measured (item 4) [from items 1 to 3; proposed].

\textit{The same equations give more.} By equation~(\ref{eq:rule}) a long pattern of light moves at $c(n)$ of equation~(\ref{eq:light}), slower near matter by twice the rate's share [derived from the rule's linear form, the remainder's term dropped; counted on a row (Section~\ref{sec:run})]. Where the content changes slowly, light bends toward matter and is late, to first order, $2 : s$ against the clock, the known $2 : 1$ as top nears bottom [the form derived; the ratio the definition's; not counted]. A short pattern of light feels the cube, equation~(\ref{eq:slow}), and the light of distant bursts, seen to 13~TeV \cite{lhaaso2023b} and showing no such slowing \cite{acciari2020,vasileiou2013,lhaaso2024}, puts the tick of time under about $2 \times 10^{-34}$~s in every way the board may lie, three bursts in three directions leaving no diagonal to hide in, and under $2.6 \times 10^{-35}$~s on an axis ($2 \times 10^{-36}$~s by the tightest bound) [derived from the rule's linear form; the bound measured; the form a prediction].

\textit{Why do we propose it?} We assume the world is a very large number of events, each speaking only with its neighbours and following one rule, for us the step of equation~(\ref{eq:rule}). Economy is our reason, not evidence. On a board built so, the loss sits where the click wiped, and that nature's arrow sits there is the proposal.

\textit{What is different here.} Earlier pictures of a discrete world \cite{zuse1969,fredkin1990,wolfram2002,thooft2016} run many rules, or state the programme and leave the rule open; here one rule, equation~(\ref{eq:rule}), for light and matter, with gravity's content in its weights, is written in full, run (Section~\ref{sec:run}) and set against what is seen, and every statement above carries its status. Collapse models put in a rate and a length of nature by hand \cite{grw1986}, or set the time by gravity's own energy \cite{penrose1996}; here the chance of each outcome is a count of the board's numbers, equation~(\ref{eq:chance}), one draw, one whole piece written back, and what is put in by hand is the click, assumption 8, with the strength $g$ and the scale $M$ of equation~(\ref{eq:weight}) and the toss of equation~(\ref{eq:chance}): the detector's numbers and its draw, not a rate or a length of nature. Gravity is not a field added beside the rule: it is a kind of the same rule, its content a count held at the Node, laid by us and moved by nothing in this paper; the count enters the step through the weights $R_j$ and $S$ of Section~\ref{sec:board}, and one rate, equation~(\ref{eq:rate}), composed unit by unit, slows the clock, equation~(\ref{eq:turn}), and light, equation~(\ref{eq:light}), in the ratio $2 : s$, the definition's powers of the rate. A body would be a pattern that holds itself together on the board; that needs the binding kind conjectured in Section~\ref{sec:board}.

\textit{What is open.} The detector is placed by us, its reset after a click and the keeping of its record ours too, outside the board; in nature the detector is a body on the board and keeps the rule, so nothing it does outruns light, and what it does in detail is not built here. The click's weights of equation~(\ref{eq:weight}), put in by hand, are not the piece's shares: they do not add up to one over the detectors, so where together they exceed what is left of the piece, equation~(\ref{eq:chance}) splits it between them in proportion, and a detector's presence changes another's rate within a stretch (Section~\ref{sec:run}): the one toss at the stretch's end sums every detector's weight in equation~(\ref{eq:chance}), by the books, outside the board, as the count's fall is (Section~\ref{sec:click}). So the click as put in has a global part, the books, which read and act on every detector at once, and that is where the far effects come from; the bound of one step per tick is the board's and holds for everything on it, numbers, patterns and the wiping, not for the click's books, which stand outside it. Nature's detectors too share one piece, but each takes it at its own rate whether or not the other stands; the board's do not. The mend is a weight that is the detector's share of the piece, the detectors' shares and the no-click share adding up to one; we have not done it here, and Section~\ref{sec:run}'s counts over seeds show the fault as it stands. The wiping carries the click's one bit at the bound, $\sqrt3\,c$ along a row, to a detector in its path; nature shows no signal above $c$; whether a body of the board, not a detector we place, could read the wiping's arrival we have not asked of the board. The bending and the delay of light near matter are not counted on the board. How a mass's content falls off from Node to Node is not set here, so whether a mass of nature shuts light in is not shown. What a moving body reads with a ruler of its own, the two-way sameness that was measured \cite{michelson1887}, and what it reads of light from one side, and what a body standing in the content reads of light with its own clock and its own ruler, are not shown here; the one-side reading depends on the matter pair, and we do not settle it here. Which pattern of the board a clock of nature is we do not say; a single pattern heavier than about $10^{-17}$~kg would, at the tick's bound, turn past a quarter swing per tick, which equation~(\ref{eq:turn}) forbids: at the empty rate $\cos(\mathrm{turn}_0)$ is top over bottom, between 0 and 1; whether such a body is one pattern or many is not shown here. Whether a body keeps its own record we do not know. A classical piece is as exclusive; whether the tie gives the correlations that in nature tell entanglement from a shared piece we have not tested.

A click picks one outcome. The step keeps the board's momentum of equation~(\ref{eq:momentum}), exact at one rate without the remainder and bounded each tick with it; the click writes into the taker four fifths of what the half that reached it carried, as its two Nodes read it and as far as it can hold it, and the wiping's cut into the other half is where the board loses momentum (Section~\ref{sec:run}); the count of pieces the books keep. A click gives one whole piece to one taker, so a second detector's chance for it is zero from that tick, wherever it stands: that is the count's doing; in the run the count fell to zero and no second click came. The other outcomes tied to the chosen one are cancelled by the count in whole pieces. The tie itself, two detectors bound by one piece, is what we call entanglement on the board.
