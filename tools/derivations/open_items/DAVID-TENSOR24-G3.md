## 1. In clocks

- **The hairspring.** A family's hand at a Node-clock is pulled only sideways, by its six neighbours, as light's is. It becomes a clock, with a beat of its own (its mass, decision 37), where a hairspring inside its own Node-clock pulls it back toward 0. The hairspring's stiffness is the coupling family's held hand there, k, times a pull per count, g.
- **The held hand's own spring.** The held hand stands wound at n because a spring of its own, kappa, pulls it back to n. Laid off n, it beats by itself, at kappa's beat: a held boson. On one Node-clock it keeps beating. On a board each gear's hands are also pulled by their neighbours, so the gears' beats spread into a band, faster for waves that change from Node-clock to Node-clock; a held hand whose beat is faster than twice the band's slowest hands its swing on to pairs of gear waves running apart, and does not keep beating alone (3, 4).
- **The stop.** A spring that stiffens as it winds, lam (the four-leg term), stops a large swing from running away. It pulls the gear alone, never the held hand. A large swing still winds the held hand down, since the hairspring pulls on the held hand as the held hand pulls on the gear; past 0 the hairspring pushes the gear out instead of in. The stop takes over what the hairspring loses; at its least it takes over that and no more. Two things move a large swing's beat off a small swing's. A stop stiffer than its least adds a stiffening of its own, and the beat rises. A held hand that follows the swing down must move as well, which makes the gear heavier, and the beat falls. So a gear's beat stays at its small swing's between a floor (a swing of about a count a round, board2mass 3.3) and the smaller of two swings: where the stop's excess over its least tells, and where following costs the held hand as much motion as the gear's own swing. A held hand laid off its valley, one whose own beat is near twice the gear's, or one too slack to keep following, beats with the gear irregularly, and one run then says little (3). The held hand's sign sets none of this. A large swing is many quanta in one gear, so what its beat means in the clicks is section 4's.
- **What the clock fixes, and what it leaves.** The stop must outpull what the hairspring loses as the held hand winds down: lam at or above g^2 / (2 kappa) for one gear on the hairspring, with every pull and every stop positive (a negative pull is a spring that pushes out, with no beat) and the held hand's own spring pulling it back to n from either side, however far, and to no other count. Several gears on one held hand all wind it down together, so a stop on each gear alone must be as many times stiffer, and then a gear swinging alone has a ceiling; one stop on all their swings together needs only the one gear's least and keeps every gear near its small swing's beat below the held hand's own scale, with the held hand near its valley. Nothing else is fixed: the pull g, the held hand's spring kappa, the stop's value at or above its least and its form (one stop on all the gears, or one on each), how the held hand pulls (through its count, or through its count's square), how many counts make n, and which gears hang on the hairspring.
