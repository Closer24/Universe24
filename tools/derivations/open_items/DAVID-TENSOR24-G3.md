## 1. In clocks

- **The hairspring.** A family's hand at a Node-clock is pulled only sideways, by its six neighbours, as light's is. It becomes a clock, with a beat of its own (its mass, decision 37), where a hairspring inside its own Node-clock pulls it back toward 0. - **The held hand's own spring.** The held hand stands wound at n because a spring of its own, kappa, pulls it back to n. Laid off n, it beats by itself, at kappa's beat: a held boson.
- **The stop.** A spring that stiffens as it winds, lam (the four-leg term), stops a large swing from running away. It pulls the gear alone, never the held hand. A large swing still winds the held hand down, since the hairspring pulls on the held hand as the held hand pulls on the gear; past 0 the hairspring pushes the gear out instead of in. The stop takes over what the hairspring loses; at its least it takes over that and no more. Two things move the beat off the mass at a large swing. A stop stiffer than its least adds a stiffening of its own, and the beat rises. A held hand that follows the swing down must move as well, which makes the gear heavier, and the beat falls. So the clock's mass stands between a floor (a swing of about a count a round, board2mass 3.3) and a ceiling at the smaller of two swings: where the stop's excess over its least tells, and where following costs the held hand as much motion as the gear's own swing.
